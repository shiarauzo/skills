## Talent / employer-brand pitch

Use when the recipient is HR, talent, or an exec, or when the event's audience is the
product (a women-in-tech hackathon, a senior-engineer summit). See Example 4 in
`examples.md`.

Five differences from the default:

1. **Reframe in the first line, before identity.** `I want to share an opportunity that
   is much more about employer brand and talent pipeline than sponsorship.` The recipient
   files the email before reading paragraph two — decide the folder for them.
2. **Numbered structure instead of prose.** This email gets forwarded internally to
   recruiting and finance. Structure survives forwarding; prose does not.
3. **Sections:** Strategic Objective → Proposed Activation → What They Gain → Next Step.
4. **Activations are talent-shaped**, not credit-shaped: a lightning talk on how they
   ship, a talent fast-track with access to the top 20–30 builders, an office-hours
   mentor room, a private roundtable.
5. **Kill the charity frame explicitly** when the event centers an underrepresented
   group: `This would not be CSR-driven. It would be recruiting and brand positioning.`
   Without this the email lands in the diversity-budget queue, which is small and slow.
   With it, it lands in recruiting, which is neither.

Close by proposing a working call with a named agenda (scope, focus areas, who
participates) rather than "would this be interesting?".

**The price still goes in the email.** Older talent pitches left budget for the call;
that costs a round trip and stalls internal approval. State the ladder — the ask, one
rung up, and a no-cash path — then use the call for scope and people. See "Always name
the price" in SKILL.md.

## Warm intro from a teammate

Use when someone on the sender's side already met the recipient — a conference booth, a
meetup, a mutual introduction. This is not cold email and should not read like one.

Three differences:

1. **Name the meeting in the second sentence**, with the teammate's first name and the
   event. `{Teammate} met you at {event} and I said I would pick it up from here.`
   It answers "who is this" before the recipient has to wonder.
2. **Skip the warmth line and the proof-of-use opener.** The meeting is the credential.
3. **Ask sooner.** A warm thread earns the number in the body rather than at the end.

If the contact address is a role inbox (`marketing@`, `partnerships@`) rather than a
person, get the name from whoever made the introduction before sending. A generic
greeting on a warm follow-up wastes the introduction — the validator flags `Hi there` as
an error for this reason.

## Follow-ups

Never resend the pitch. Each follow-up must carry something new.

- **Follow-up 1 (5–7 days).** Three or four sentences. Add one new fact that did not
  exist when the first email was sent: another partner confirmed, applications crossed a
  number, a track filled up. Re-ask in one line.
- **Follow-up 2 (7–10 days later).** Shrink the ask. `Totally understand if Main Partner
  isn't the fit this cycle — would credits for teams and a judge be easier?` A smaller
  yes now is worth more than a maybe.
- **Follow-up 3 — the close-out.** One or two sentences, no ask, no guilt.
  `Closing the loop on this one. If the timing is wrong, happy to come back for the next
  edition — just let me know.` This one gets replies surprisingly often, and it protects
  the relationship for the next event.

Never guilt, never `just bumping this`, never `following up on my previous email`.

## Smaller asks

When Main Partner is implausible — small company, no marketing budget, no history of
sponsoring — lead with the smaller ask instead of laddering down from a no:

- Credits for teams (cheapest yes in software; often approved by one person)
- A prize for the best project using their product
- One mentor or judge for a few hours
- A pre-event workshop, no money involved
- Amplification only: they post about it, you tag them

Any of these creates the relationship. Next edition, the Main Partner ask has history
behind it.

## Batch outreach

When pitching a prospect list:

1. Research each company separately (`research.md`) — this is the step people skip and
   it is the whole game.
2. Write each email in full. Never a template with `{{company}}` placeholders; the fit
   thesis and activation menu must differ per company.
3. Vary the opening move. Ten identical first paragraphs is a pattern that gets noticed
   if two recipients ever compare, and DevRel people do compare.
4. Derive the per-prospect deck URL for each (`/deck/{company-slug}`).
5. Deliver as separate blocks with a one-line fit-thesis note above each, so the sender
   can spot the weak ones before sending.
6. Flag any prospect where the research showed weak fit rather than shipping a forced
   pitch.

## Spanish-language prospects

For LatAm companies where the recipient writes in Spanish, keep the same skeleton and
the same restraint. Adjust only these:

- Use `tú`, not `usted`, when pitching startups and DevRel. `usted` reads corporate and
  distant in this context.
- The negation move works identically: `no es solo poner el logo`, `no son pitch decks`.
- Do not translate the activation nouns that are English in practice: `credits`,
  `starter kit`, `track`, `demo`. Forcing them into Spanish reads translated.
- Keep the event name, deck URL, and tier names in English.

## Subject lines

Six to nine words. Concrete, no hype, no clickbait, no question marks.

Good:
- `[Event] — Voice Agents track for [Company]`
- `300 builders across the region, August 1 — [Company] as Main Partner`
- `[Event]: employer brand and talent, not sponsorship`

Bad: `Partnership opportunity`, `Quick question`, `Collaboration?`,
`Amazing opportunity for {Company}` — all read as bulk mail.

Naming the company in the subject is worth it. Naming the *specific track or role* you
are offering them is worth more.
